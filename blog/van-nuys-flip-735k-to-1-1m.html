$735K to $1.1M: Anatomy of a Van Nuys Flip

A house on Leadwell Street in Van Nuys sold for $735,000 last year. It was renovated and relisted at $1,099,000. That is a spread of roughly $364,000, and understanding where that money went is the most useful market lesson I can give you right now.

I shoot a lot of flips and people always ask the same thing: is that a rip off, or is that fair? The honest answer is that it depends entirely on line items most buyers never see.

What the spread is not

It is not $364,000 of profit. That is the number people jump to and it is wrong every time.

Out of that spread comes the renovation itself, the carrying costs for however many months the money was tied up, the financing, permits, both sides of the transaction costs on the way in and on the way out, and the commissions. Flippers are usually borrowing at rates well above a normal mortgage, and every month of holding eats the margin.

There is also the risk premium. Nobody knew this house would come back at $1.1M when they bought it at $735K. If the market had moved against them or the renovation had uncovered foundation work, that spread would look very different.

What the money visibly bought

From what I saw, the work is not cosmetic. Vaulted white oak ceilings, which means structural work, not a paint job. A Topaz quartz waterfall island, which is a real kitchen, not a countertop swap. And a backyard sized for a pool or an ADU.

That last one is the item I would underline. In Los Angeles right now, a lot with room for an accessory dwelling unit is not just a nice yard, it is optionality. It is a future rental, a place for family, or a resale story for the next buyer. Buyers are pricing that in, and California's ADU rules are why a big flat backyard reads differently than it did ten years ago.

What it says about the Valley market

Three things.

Renovated inventory is scarce. The premium here exists because most buyers cannot or will not take on a project. Between contractor costs, permit timelines, and the fact that renovation money is not financeable the way purchase money is, move in ready carries a real premium.

The gap between rough and finished is wide. Wide enough to attract professional capital, which tells you something. When flippers are active in a neighborhood, they have run numbers on the direction of that neighborhood and are betting on it.

Van Nuys is being repriced. A house asking $1.1M in Van Nuys would have been a conversation a few years ago. The Valley's price map is moving outward from the Studio City and Sherman Oaks core, and neighborhoods that were never in the conversation are entering it.

What to do with this if you are a buyer

If you have the appetite and the cash, the unrenovated version of this house is where the value is. You are buying the same land and the same location at a large discount, and the difference is work.

If you do not have the appetite, that is completely fine, and the premium you pay for finished is the price of not living in a construction zone for eight months. Just go in knowing what you are paying for.

What you should not do is assume the finished number is inflated simply because you know the previous sale price. Zillow makes that history visible now, and I watch buyers anchor to it and talk themselves out of fair deals.

If you are a seller

Know which buyer you are selling to before you decide whether to renovate. A partial renovation is often the worst outcome, because you spend real money and still sell to the buyer who wanted a project. Either take it all the way, or sell it honestly as is and price for the buyer who wants that.
