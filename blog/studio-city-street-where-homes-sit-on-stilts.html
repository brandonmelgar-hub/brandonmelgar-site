Studio City · Alta-Avenida Loop

The Studio City Street Where the Homes Sit on Stilts

There is a pocket in the Studio City hills where some of the homes are literally standing on stilts, steel and concrete columns bored straight down into the hillside. It is called the Alta-Avenida Loop, homes here run around $3.0M, and it is minutes from Ventura Boulevard.

I drive this pocket often and it still gets me. You come around a curve and there is a house cantilevered out over a canyon, the living room floating in open air with nothing under it but posts and hillside.

Why anyone builds this way

Studio City ran out of flat land a long time ago. What was left was slope, and slope is difficult to build on. You have two choices: cut into the hill and set the house back, or reach out over the drop on columns.

Cutting into the hill costs you the view, the thing you paid the premium for. So builders went the other way. Drill caissons deep into bedrock, put the structure on top, and let the house extend into the air where the view is.

The result is that a fairly ordinary square footage buys you something no flat lot can offer: Coldwater Canyon out one side, the Valley out the other, and nobody directly behind you.

What you are actually buying

The view, and the privacy that comes with a street where the houses are stacked at different elevations rather than side by side. Homes here are single family on extreme hillside angles, and the buyer tends to be someone who wants a genuine hillside address without leaving the Valley.

Being minutes from Ventura Boulevard is the part people underrate. Plenty of places in Los Angeles offer canyon seclusion. Very few put it this close to a real commercial street.

The honest part, and it matters more here than almost anywhere

A hillside home on caissons is a different purchase than a house on a flat lot, and I would not let anyone buy one without going in clear eyed.

Get a structural engineer, not just a general inspector. I recommend three inspections to every buyer, general, sewer, and termite. On a hillside home on columns, add a fourth. A general inspector is a generalist by design and is not going to evaluate caissons, retaining walls, or how the structure is tied into the slope.

Drainage is the whole ballgame on a hillside. Water moving through soil is what causes movement. Ask where the water goes in a hard rain, and look at the retaining walls and the grading with that question in mind.

Ask for the permit history. These are engineered structures. You want to see that the engineering was done, inspected, and signed off, especially on anything added later. A deck cantilevered over a canyon without a permit is not a charming quirk, it is a liability you inherit.

Expect insurance and financing to ask more questions than they would on a flat lot in Colfax Meadows.

None of that is a reason to avoid the pocket. It is a reason to buy carefully. A properly engineered hillside home that has been maintained is a fantastic thing to own, and the scarcity is real, nobody is manufacturing more hillside.

Who it is not for

If you want a flat backyard for kids or dogs, this is the wrong pocket and I would rather tell you now than after four weekends of showings. Hillside living generally means terraced outdoor space, decks instead of lawn, and stairs.

Where it sits among the 22

Studio City is not one market, it is 22 pockets, and the difference between two of them can be millions of dollars and an entirely different daily life. Alta-Avenida sits at the higher end at roughly $3.0M, a completely different product from a condo in The Grove or a townhome in Studio Village.
